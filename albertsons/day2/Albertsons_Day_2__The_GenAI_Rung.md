<overview>
This is Dr. Anand Jayaraman's Day 2 lecture deck for the "Enterprise AI Architecture Intensive" delivered to Albertsons (InsightVerge, a Protiviti partner company). It is the second day of a 3.5-day executive/architect-level training program; Day 1 covered classical analytics, ML forecasting, and simulation. Day 2's subject is the "GenAI rung of the ladder": how large language models actually work under the hood, why they need to be grounded with retrieval before they can be trusted on enterprise data, and how to right-size a deployment (prompting vs. RAG vs. fine-tuning, and model size/quantization choices) instead of defaulting to the biggest, most expensive model.

29 slides, no embedded video. The deck is built around one running example introduced on slide 3 and referenced repeatedly afterward: a category manager asking "Can I run the same promotion next month, under the new vendor funding policy and the loyalty program terms?" — a question none of Day 1's tools (rules engines, classical ML) can even read, because it requires understanding prose policy documents.

Structure, in the order the deck actually presents it:
- **Opening (slides 1-4).** Title slide, the day's three-part roadmap, the running example question, and a "triage checkpoint" verdict: GenAI is the right tool here, used in a hybrid way (the LLM interprets and cites; a classical ML forecast stays a forecast).
- **Segment 1: What an LLM Actually Is (slides 5-12).** A mechanistic build-up: naive word-list sentiment analysis fails on context, so tokenization and Word2Vec-style embeddings are introduced, which still fail on polysemy (one word, one vector), which motivates attention, which stacked at scale is a Transformer, which through pretraining plus supervised fine-tuning plus RLHF becomes ChatGPT. The segment closes on the "Shoggoth with a smiley face" illustration and three operating rules (never a database, never a calculator, never a policy engine) that justify everything that follows.
- **Bridge: Why RAG (slide 13).** A single condensed slide, "The Model Doesn't Know Two Things" (frozen training cutoff; never seen your private data), that motivates retrieval directly. Note: this deck does not include separate slides on structured-output "airlock" mechanics, real-world hallucination case studies (e.g., law-firm citation failures), or a dedicated cost/token-economics slide — those grounding/governance topics that appeared in earlier drafts of this deck were trimmed for time, and slide 13 is the sole bridge from mechanics into RAG.
- **Segment 3: Enterprise RAG (slides 14-20).** Opens with a real RAG architecture sequence diagram, then walks retrieval production concerns in a deliberately split order: chunking (the basic build decision) is kept separate from filtering for access control at scale (the production/security escalation), then retrieval mode selection and the groundedness "exit criterion," then measuring quality with RAGAS, then the two things that break RAG once it's live in production (stale data, cost), then a closing callback to Day 1's "Empty Pantry" problem: RAG only works as well as the documents underneath it.
- **Right-Sizing (slides 21-28).** No dedicated segment-opener or scaling-laws/Chinchilla slide in this version — it moves straight from the RAG segment's close into a small-model-vs-frontier-model comparison, then a three-way prompting/RAG/fine-tuning decision table, then fine-tuning mechanics (why fine-tune, the cost problem, PEFT's fix, storage economics), a real-world case study (Thomson Reuters), quantization, and a closing decision rule slide with explicit "justified when / rejected when" criteria for fine-tuning.
- **Closing (slide 29).** A single five-point "what you're actually guarding against" slide: performing (AI theater), skipping the boundary test, no right-sizing discipline, undocumented rejected options, and no owner on the model's output. This replaces what earlier drafts handled as a separate workshop exercise plus honest-close plus lab-transition sequence — in this final version it is compressed into one closing slide.

Text content: RAG • HOW IT'S BUILT. Title: "Chunking: The First Decision in RAG." Subtitle: "Before a single document can be retrieved, it has to be cut into pieces. How you cut it decides how well it can ever be found again." THE SIZE TRADE-OFF: larger chunks (more context, slower, drags in irrelevance) vs. smaller chunks (precise and fast, but meaning leaks out; sweet spot ~300-500 tokens, never split mid-sentence). Three-step ladder: (1) Fixed-size — cut every N tokens, cheapest/crudest, ignores meaning. (2) Sliding-window/recursive — overlap chunks, split on structure so context survives the cut.
